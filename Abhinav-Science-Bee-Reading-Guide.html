LESSON 1 · Weeks 7–8 · Read first: stellar life cycles and the three kinds of solar activity

Stars, the Sun and the universe

How a star lives and dies

Gravity pulls gas and dust together in a nebula to form a protostar. Once its core is hot enough for sustained hydrogen fusion, it becomes a main-sequence star. Fusion converts hydrogen into helium and releases energy. A Sun-like star later expands into a red giant, sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and leaves a white dwarf. A sufficiently massive star can undergo core collapse and a supernova, leaving a neutron star or black hole. A planetary nebula has nothing to do with forming planets.

Read the Sun from the inside out

Core → radiative zone → convection zone → photosphere → chromosphere → corona. The photosphere is the visible surface; the corona is the outer atmosphere. Sunspots look dark because they are cooler than their surroundings. The solar wind is a continuing flow of charged particles. A flare is a burst of radiation; a coronal mass ejection (CME) sends plasma and magnetic field into space. A flare and a CME can occur together.

Reading the sky

An official constellation is a region of sky. An asterism is a recognizable star pattern: the Big Dipper sits within Ursa Major, while the Summer Triangle uses stars in three constellations. Stars that look close in a pattern may be far apart in space. A black hole’s event horizon is the boundary beyond which light cannot escape to distant observers.

Evidence for the early universe

The Big Bang model describes expansion from an early hot, dense state. Cosmological redshift is stretching of light as space expands. The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is light released when the early universe became transparent, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. Gravitational lensing is the bending of light by mass; it can reveal mass that emits little or no light. Dark matter is inferred from gravitational effects. Dark energy is the name for whatever drives the observed acceleration of cosmic expansion.

Close the notes and explain

What determines the broad path a star follows after the main sequence?

Its mass. The Sun is expected to end as a white dwarf.

Which clue points to a CME rather than a flare?

A large cloud of plasma and magnetic field ejected from the Sun.

LESSON 2 · Weeks 8 and 14 · Read first: accretion, differentiation and evidence

Early Earth and the solar system

About 4.6 billion years ago, a cloud of gas and dust collapsed into a rotating disk. The Sun formed near the centre; solids in the disk collided and accumulated into larger bodies. This growth is accretion. In early Earth, denser material sank while lighter material rose, helping produce a metal-rich core and rocky outer layers. This separation is differentiation.

Earth’s ancient history is reconstructed from rocks, minerals, meteorites and lunar samples. Some models propose a period of intense early impacts, often called the Late Heavy Bombardment. Whether there was a sharp spike is debated. Earth’s water may have come from multiple sources, including water-bearing material incorporated during formation. The exact path from nonliving chemistry to the first life is unresolved. Keep hypotheses separate from established observations.

Pangaea was a supercontinent that later broke apart as tectonic plates moved. Latitude measures degrees north or south of the equator; longitude measures degrees east or west of the prime meridian. Toronto is north of the equator and west of the prime meridian.

Close the notes and explain

What is the difference between accretion and differentiation?

Accretion builds a body from smaller pieces; differentiation separates materials within it.

What reference lines define latitude and longitude?

The equator and prime meridian, respectively.

LESSON 3 · Week 9 · Background notes for inaccessible lesson PDFs

Matter, properties and phase changes

In a solid, particles vibrate near fixed positions. In a liquid, they stay close but can move past each other. In a gas, they spread through the available space. Plasma contains freely moving charged particles. In a Bose–Einstein condensate, certain particles at extremely low temperatures occupy the same quantum state.

ChangeDirection
Melting / freezingSolid ⇄ liquid
Vaporization / condensationLiquid ⇄ gas
Sublimation / depositionSolid ⇄ gas

Evaporation occurs at a liquid’s surface; boiling produces vapour bubbles throughout it. A physical change alters form or state while preserving chemical identity. A chemical change produces different substances. Colour change, bubbles or temperature change can be clues, but they are not proof by themselves: boiling water bubbles without forming a new substance.

Mass and volume depend on sample size; density is mass divided by volume and does not depend on the size of a uniform sample under the same conditions. A 36 g sample occupying 12 cm³ has a density of 3 g/cm³. Flammability and the tendency to rust are chemical properties.

Close the notes and explain

Frost grows directly from water vapour. What is the change?

Deposition, gas to solid.

Why can bubbles appear in both a physical and a chemical change?

Boiling produces vapour of the same substance; a reaction can generate a new gas.

LESSON 4 · Weeks 10–11 · Read first: proton count, electron changes and what bonds predict

Atoms, the periodic table and bonding

The nucleus contains positive protons and uncharged neutrons; negative electrons occupy the space around it. Atomic number counts protons. Mass number counts protons plus neutrons. Isotopes differ in neutrons, and ions differ in electrons. For example, chlorine-37 with a −1 charge has 17 protons, 20 neutrons and 18 electrons. The decimal atomic mass on most periodic tables is an isotope-weighted average, not the mass number of a particular atom. A radioactive isotope’s half-life is the time for half of a large sample’s unstable nuclei to decay.

Groups are columns and periods are rows. Group 1 contains the alkali metals (except hydrogen); group 2, alkaline earth metals; groups 13–16 include the boron, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen families; group 17 contains halogens; group 18 contains noble gases. The lanthanides and actinides are usually shown below the main table. Across a period, atomic radius generally decreases while first ionization energy and electronegativity generally increase, with exceptions; down a group the general trends reverse. Ionization energy measures how hard it is to remove an electron; electronegativity describes attraction for shared electrons in a bond.

BondingWhat holds it together?Example or clue
IonicOpposite charges attract in an ion lattice.NaCl conducts when molten or dissolved because ions can move.
CovalentAtoms share electron pairs. One, two or three shared pairs make single, double or triple bonds.H₂O has polar bonds; many small molecular substances conduct poorly.
MetallicElectrons are shared across a metal lattice and can move through it.Copper conducts electricity and can be drawn into wire.

A compound has chemically combined elements in fixed proportions. H₂O and H₂O₂ contain the same elements but have different proportions and properties. The sheet assigns memorizing elements 1–30 and making flash cards for 1–60. Learn number → symbol → name, then reverse the cue.

1–30: 1 H hydrogen · 2 He helium · 3 Li lithium · 4 Be beryllium · 5 B boron · 6 C carbon · 7 N nitrogen · 8 O oxygen · 9 F fluorine · 10 Ne neon · 11 Na sodium · 12 Mg magnesium · 13 Al aluminum · 14 Si silicon · 15 P phosphorus · 16 S sulfur · 17 Cl chlorine · 18 Ar argon · 19 K potassium · 20 Ca calcium · 21 Sc scandium · 22 Ti titanium · 23 V vanadium · 24 Cr chromium · 25 Mn manganese · 26 Fe iron · 27 Co cobalt · 28 Ni nickel · 29 Cu copper · 30 Zn zinc.

Stretch: The four fundamental interactions are gravity, electromagnetism, the strong interaction and the weak interaction. Ordinary chemical bonding is electromagnetic; it does not change the nuclei.

Close the notes and explain

An atom has 12 protons and 10 electrons. What is it?

Mg²⁺. Twelve protons identify magnesium; the two missing electrons give a +2 charge.

Why does solid NaCl conduct poorly while molten NaCl conducts?

The ions are fixed in the solid lattice but mobile in the melt.

LESSON 5 · Week 11 · Read first: structures, biomolecules and polymers

Carbon in life and materials

Carbon can form four covalent bonds and can bond to other carbon atoms. This supports chains, branches, rings and multiple bonds. A hydrocarbon contains only carbon and hydrogen. Isomers have the same molecular formula but differ in how their atoms are arranged. Carbon dioxide is usually classified as inorganic; graphite is elemental carbon. Carbon’s presence alone does not make a substance organic.

GroupWhat to recognize
CarbohydratesSugars and starches provide or store energy; cellulose helps build plant cell walls.
LipidsFats store energy; phospholipids help form membranes.
ProteinsAmino acid chains fold into shapes; enzymes speed reactions.
Nucleic acidsDNA stores hereditary information; RNA helps use it, among other roles.

Monomers are small building units; a polymer is built from repeated units. Ethylene can form polyethylene. Petroleum can be separated into fractions with different boiling ranges and used as a source of chemical feedstocks. The shape and arrangement of molecules help explain why different plastics and fibres behave differently. Carbon is especially versatile in Earth’s biology; this does not establish that other kinds of life are impossible.

Close the notes and explain

Two compounds share the formula C₄H₁₀ but one chain branches. What are they?

Structural isomers: same formula, different connectivity.

What molecular feature often determines whether a protein can do its job?

Its three-dimensional shape, which depends partly on its amino acid sequence.

LESSON 6 · Week 12 · Read first: what dissolves, what settles and what pH measures

Solutions, acids and alloys

MixtureWhat to look forExample
SolutionUniform at molecular or ionic scale; dissolved particles do not settle.Salt water
ColloidDispersed particles can scatter light and do not readily settle.Milk
SuspensionLarger particles may settle or be filtered out.Muddy water

A solvent dissolves a solute. Concentration measures how much solute is present in a given amount of solution. Solubility is how much can dissolve at given conditions; saturation means that limit has been reached. Stirring can speed dissolving without changing equilibrium solubility. Many solids dissolve better as temperature rises, while gases are often less soluble in warmer liquids. Higher gas pressure above a liquid generally increases the amount of that gas dissolved.

In water, acids increase hydronium concentration and bases can accept protons or increase hydroxide. At about 25°C, pH 7 is neutral; lower is acidic and higher basic. Each pH unit represents a tenfold change in hydrogen ion activity. Acid strength describes how fully an acid ionizes; concentration describes how much is present. Strong and concentrated are different ideas. Acid–base neutralization can produce salt and water.

Alloys are mixtures that include a metal. Brass is mainly copper and zinc; bronze is classically copper and tin; steel is principally iron with carbon. Changing the composition can change hardness, resistance to corrosion or other properties.

Close the notes and explain

Which has more hydrogen ion activity: pH 3 or pH 5, and by how much?

pH 3, by a factor of 100.

Does stirring change the amount of solute that can eventually dissolve?

Usually no, if temperature and other conditions stay the same; it changes the rate.

LESSON 7 · Weeks 13–14 · Start here for the current assignment

Earth’s spheres, rocks and minerals

The geosphere is solid Earth, the hydrosphere is Earth’s water, the atmosphere is its gases and the biosphere is living organisms. The cryosphere is the frozen-water part often tracked separately. When a glacier grinds bedrock, the cryosphere interacts with the geosphere; meltwater can then move the sediment.

A mineral is a naturally occurring inorganic solid with an ordered crystal structure and characteristic composition. A rock is a solid aggregate typically made of one or more minerals. Identify minerals by several clues: hardness, streak (powder colour), lustre and whether they break along planes (cleavage) or irregularly (fracture). Mohs hardness ranks scratch resistance; it is not an equal-interval scale. From softest to hardest: talc, gypsum, calcite, fluorite, apatite, orthoclase feldspar, quartz, topaz, corundum, diamond.

Rock typeHow it formsClues and examples
IgneousMolten rock cools and solidifies.Intrusive granite cools slowly and often has visible crystals. Extrusive basalt generally cools faster and has fine grains. Obsidian is volcanic glass.
SedimentarySediment accumulates, then compacts and cements; some rocks form by precipitation or accumulation of biological material.Sandstone, shale, limestone, rock salt. Beds, fossils or ripple marks may record an older environment.
MetamorphicHeat, pressure and/or fluids change existing rock while it stays largely solid.Slate, schist and gneiss are often foliated. Limestone can become marble; quartz sandstone can become quartzite.

Rock cycle: weathering breaks rock down; erosion moves the pieces; deposition drops them. Compaction and cementation can turn sediment into rock. Heat and pressure can transform any existing rock. Melting creates magma, and cooling makes igneous rock. There is no mandatory order. Granitic magma tends to be richer in silica than basaltic magma; greater silica content generally increases viscosity.

Close the notes and explain

A rock has rounded pebbles cemented into a matrix. How did it form?

The pebbles were deposited and cemented; the rock is sedimentary conglomerate.

Why is marble metamorphic rather than igneous?

Limestone recrystallizes while solid; it does not have to melt.
