Red Set Science Study Guide

This guide is built from the official 2026-27 Red Set Science Study Guide: seven sections of core facts, followed by the guide's 30 Terms for Further Study. IAC's own warning applies: many guide facts appear in the Red set's questions, but the majority of tournament questions are not referenced in the guide.

Each topic follows the same chain: Identify (what it is), Explain (how it works), Significance (why it matters), Connections (links to other topics in the set), and Confusables (the trap answer, and how to tell them apart).

Biology & Anatomy

Rabies

  • Identify: Rabies is a fatal viral disease of the nervous system, also called hydrophobia.
  • Explain: The virus spreads in the saliva of an infected mammal, usually through a bite. From the wound it travels slowly along nerves toward the brain, which is why symptoms can take weeks or months to appear. Once it reaches the brain it causes agitation, confusion, excess saliva (the famous frothing at the mouth), and painful throat spasms when the patient tries to drink - the "fear of water" that gives hydrophobia its name.
  • Significance: Rabies is feared because once symptoms appear it is almost always fatal, yet it is nearly always preventable if a vaccine is given soon after the bite, before the virus reaches the brain. Louis Pasteur developed the first rabies vaccine in 1885.
  • Connections: It is caused by a virus, which contrasts with the bacteria below; the vaccine connects it to the immune system and to white blood cells in the Terms section.
  • Confusables: Hydrophobia is a symptom and old name for rabies, not a separate disease. Tetanus also follows animal bites and wounds, but it is bacterial and causes muscle stiffness rather than fear of water.

Tyrannosaurus rex

  • Identify: Tyrannosaurus rex, "tyrant lizard king," was a giant bipedal meat-eating dinosaur of Late Cretaceous North America.
  • Explain: T. rex grew to about 12 meters long with a massive skull, banana-sized serrated teeth, and one of the strongest bites of any land animal - but arms so small they had only two fingers. It walked on two legs as a theropod, the group that also includes the ancestors of birds. The most complete fossil, nicknamed Sue, is about 90 percent complete and sits in Chicago's Field Museum.
  • Significance: It is the most famous dinosaur in the world and a standard anchor for questions on the Cretaceous period and on apex predators.
  • Connections: T. rex lived at the end of the Jurassic's era - the Mesozoic - and died out in the extinction 66 million years ago; the trilobite in the Terms section is an earlier fossil celebrity.
  • Confusables: T. rex is Cretaceous, not Jurassic, despite Jurassic Park. It was among the largest bipedal predators in North America; the still larger Spinosaurus lived in Africa.

The heart

  • Identify: The heart is the muscular organ of the circulatory system that pumps blood around the body.
  • Explain: It works as two pumps side by side. The right side receives oxygen-poor blood from the body and sends it to the lungs; the left side receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs and drives it out to the whole body, which is why the left wall is thicker. Its four chambers are two upper atria that receive blood and two lower ventricles that eject it. A resting adult heart beats about 60 to 100 times per minute.
  • Significance: Its double-circuit design is what separates mammals and birds from animals with a single circuit.
  • Connections: It pumps the blood that white blood cells patrol in; its beat is a rhythm the brain (in the packet questions) regulates.
  • Confusables: The heart is an organ, not a muscle type of its own kind of answer - if a question asks what pumps blood, answer heart, not arteries or veins, which are vessels.

Bacteria (Salmonella and E. coli)

  • Identify: Bacteria are single-celled living organisms; Salmonella and E. coli are two that cause food poisoning.
  • Explain: A bacterium is a complete cell without a nucleus - its DNA floats in a loop inside the cell. Bacteria reproduce on their own by splitting in two, live almost everywhere, and are mostly harmless or helpful: gut bacteria aid digestion and others fix nitrogen for plants. The harmful minority, like Salmonella in undercooked poultry and eggs and certain strains of E. coli in contaminated food and water, release toxins or invade tissue.
  • Significance: Bacteria are the dividing line between living cells and viruses in most bee questions, and they are why antibiotics exist. Alexander Fleming's discovery that Penicillium mold kills bacteria led to penicillin.
  • Connections: Bacteria contrast with viruses (rabies) and with prions in the Terms section, which are not alive at all; ribosomes, also in the Terms, are the protein builders bacteria share with our own cells.
  • Confusables: Viruses are not bacteria - viruses are not cells, cannot reproduce alone, and are not killed by antibiotics. E. coli is one species of bacterium, not a synonym for bacteria.

The small intestine

  • Identify: The small intestine is the long digestive organ between the stomach and the colon (large intestine).
  • Explain: Food leaving the stomach enters its three parts in order: duodenum, jejunum, ileum. In the duodenum, juices from the pancreas and bile from the liver finish chemical digestion; along the whole length, millions of finger-like villi give it an enormous surface area, and nutrients pass through the villi walls into the blood. It is several meters long - far longer than the large intestine despite the names, which refer to width, not length.
  • Significance: This is where digestion is completed and almost all nutrient absorption happens, making it the functional center of the digestive system.
  • Connections: It receives food from the stomach and passes the remainder to the large intestine, both of which appear in the packet questions; the leaf's flat shape in Earth Science is the plant version of the same surface-area trick as villi.
  • Confusables: The large intestine (colon) mainly absorbs water and forms solid waste - if the clue says nutrient absorption or villi, the answer is small intestine.

Marsupials

  • Identify: Marsupials are the group of mammals whose young develop in a pouch - kangaroos, koalas, opossums, and wombats.
  • Explain: A marsupial baby is born extremely early, tiny and hairless, then crawls to the mother's pouch and attaches to a nipple, finishing its development there over weeks or months. Most marsupials live in Australia and nearby islands, with the opossum the familiar North American exception.
  • Significance: Marsupials are the standard mammal group defined by reproduction rather than appearance.
  • Connections: The packet's monotremes (platypus and echidnas) are the other non-placental mammal group; both connect to the mammal family tree behind many packet questions.
  • Confusables: Monotremes lay eggs; marsupials give live birth and then use a pouch. If the clue says egg-laying mammal, it is never a marsupial.

The skeleton and 206 bones

  • Identify: The adult human skeleton is the body's bony framework and consists of 206 bones.
  • Explain: Babies are born with around 270 to 300 bones and bone pieces, many of them cartilage; as a child grows, bones fuse - in the skull, spine, and elsewhere - until the adult count settles at 206. Bones support and protect the body, anchor muscles for movement, store minerals such as calcium, and, in the red marrow inside them, manufacture blood cells. The largest bone is the femur (thigh bone) and the smallest is the stapes in the ear.
  • Significance: The number 206 is one of the most asked anatomy facts at this level, and the fusion story explains why the number is not fixed at birth.
  • Connections: Bone marrow makes the white blood cells of the Terms section; the skeleton pairs with the heart and other organs across the packet's anatomy questions.
  • Confusables: Do not answer 206 for a baby, and do not confuse bones with cartilage, which is the flexible tissue in the nose, ears, and joints.

The Jurassic period

  • Identify: The Jurassic is the middle period of the Mesozoic era, the age of dinosaurs, lasting roughly 201 to 145 million years ago.
  • Explain: The Mesozoic era has three periods in order: Triassic, Jurassic, Cretaceous. In the Jurassic the supercontinent Pangaea was splitting apart and dinosaurs reached their greatest sizes - the long-necked sauropods such as Brachiosaurus and Diplodocus. The first birds, including Archaeopteryx, appeared from small feathered dinosaurs. The period is named for the Jura Mountains on the border of France and Switzerland, where its rocks were first studied.
  • Significance: "Jurassic" is the most recognized geological period name, and questions test whether you can place it between Triassic and Cretaceous and resist putting every famous dinosaur in it.
  • Connections: T. rex belongs to the following period, the Cretaceous; trilobites (Terms section) belong to the much earlier Paleozoic era, before the Mesozoic began.
  • Confusables: Jurassic is a period, Mesozoic is the era containing it. T. rex, Triceratops, and the asteroid extinction are all Cretaceous, not Jurassic.

Astronomy

Jupiter and Europa

  • Identify: Jupiter is the largest planet in the Solar System, a gas giant and the fifth planet from the Sun.
  • Explain: Jupiter is more massive than all the other planets combined. Its Great Red Spot is a storm wider than Earth that has raged for centuries. It has dozens of moons; the four largest - Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto - are treated in the Terms section. Europa's smooth icy crust almost certainly covers a deep salty ocean, kept liquid by tidal flexing from Jupiter's gravity, which makes it one of the best candidates for harboring extraterrestrial life.
  • Significance: Jupiter anchors questions on gas giants, storms, and the search for life; its gravity also shapes the asteroid belt and deflects comets.
  • Connections: Europa returns under Moons of Jupiter in the Terms; Jupiter's size contrasts with Mercury, the smallest planet, in the packet questions.
  • Confusables: Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System and is bigger than Mercury; Europa is the icy ocean moon. Saturn, not Jupiter, is the ringed planet of the standard clue.

Halley's Comet

  • Identify: Halley's Comet is the most famous periodic comet, returning about every 75 to 76 years; it was last visible from Earth in 1986.
  • Explain: A comet is a body of ice and dust on a long elliptical orbit (see Ellipse in the Terms). As it nears the Sun, heat vaporizes ice into a glowing coma and tail. Edmond Halley compared records of comets seen in 1531, 1607, and 1682, realized they were one object, and predicted its 1758 return - the first successful prediction of a comet's return, which is why it carries his name.
  • Significance: Halley's Comet is the model answer for any clue mentioning a roughly 75-year period, a 1986 sighting, or a predicted return.
  • Connections: Its orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus; comets with far longer periods are thought to come from the Oort cloud (Terms section).
  • Confusables: A comet is icy and grows a tail near the Sun; an asteroid is rocky and does not. Halley pronounced his name to rhyme roughly with "valley," but the answer only needs the name.

The speed of light

  • Identify: The speed of light in a vacuum, written c, is 299,792,458 meters per second - about 300,000 kilometers per second.
  • Explain: This is the fastest speed anything can travel. Light slows slightly in water or glass, but in vacuum its speed is a universal constant, the same for every observer, which is the foundation of relativity (Terms section). Astronomers therefore measure cosmic distances in light-years. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth. Note: the official guide misprints this figure; 299,792,458 meters per second is the correct, exact value.
  • Significance: c appears in Einstein's E = mc squared, where its square converts mass into energy, and it sets the speed limit behind time dilation.
  • Connections: Relativity and the ellipse (orbits timed by light-travel) both lean on it; light waves in the packet questions travel at this speed while sound waves crawl by comparison.
  • Confusables: The speed of sound in air is only about 343 meters per second - nearly a million times slower. Never give the sound figure for a light question.

Supernova

  • Identify: A supernova is the catastrophic explosion of a star, briefly outshining an entire galaxy.
  • Explain: There are two main routes. In one, a white dwarf - the dense leftover core of a Sun-like star - pulls matter from a companion star until it passes a critical mass limit of about 1.4 solar masses; its carbon core then ignites all at once in a runaway fusion reaction that tears the star apart. In the other route, a very massive star exhausts its fuel, its core collapses under gravity in a fraction of a second, and the rebound blasts the outer layers into space, leaving a neutron star or black hole behind.
  • Significance: Supernovae forge and scatter the heavy elements - iron, gold, and more - that planets and people are made of, and the white-dwarf type is uniform enough in brightness to measure distances across the universe.
  • Connections: The leftover clouds of a supernova are nebulae (Terms), and the collapsed core can become a pulsar or black hole, both in the packet's class notes; Henri Becquerel's radioactivity (Terms) is powered by the same nuclear physics at a smaller scale.
  • Confusables: A nova is a much smaller flare-up on a white dwarf's surface that leaves the star intact; a supernova destroys or transforms the star. Fusion runaway in a white dwarf is one cause, not the only one - core collapse is the other.

Chemistry

Gold

  • Identify: Gold is a dense, soft, yellow precious metal, chemical symbol Au.
  • Explain: Two properties explain its career. It is the most malleable metal - a single gram can be beaten into a sheet about a square meter in area. And it resists corrosion: it does not rust or tarnish in air or water, dissolving only in a special acid mixture called aqua regia, so ancient coins and jewelry survive thousands of years looking new. Its symbol Au comes from aurum, the Latin word for gold.
  • Significance: Malleability made gold workable for jewelry and coinage before modern tools, and incorruptibility made it trusted as currency - the two guide facts are one story.
  • Connections: Gold sits in the periodic table below; its density (Chemistry's next topic) is famously high, which is why prospectors could pan for it.
  • Confusables: Fool's gold is pyrite, an iron mineral that glitters but is brittle and pale. Gold's symbol is Au, not G.

Density

  • Identify: Density is the ratio of a substance's mass to its volume, symbolized by the Greek letter rho.
  • Explain: In words, density is mass divided by volume: pack more mass into the same space and density rises. It is measured in units like grams per cubic centimeter; water's density is 1 by that scale. An object floats in a liquid if its overall density is lower than the liquid's, which is why ice and cork float, why a steel ship floats (its hollow shape lowers its average density), and why Saturn could float in a big enough bathtub.
  • Significance: Density explains floating, sinking, layered liquids, and why the packet's Saturn and gold facts work; it is the first quantitative relation most Red questions use.
  • Connections: Density links to mass in the Terms section (mass is the numerator) and to convection (Terms), which is driven by density differences in fluids.
  • Confusables: Density is not weight and not mass: a kilogram of lead and a kilogram of feathers have equal mass but wildly different densities because their volumes differ.

The Bunsen burner

  • Identify: A Bunsen burner is a laboratory device that burns natural gas to make a controlled flame.
  • Explain: Gas enters at the base and mixes with air drawn in through an adjustable collar hole. With the hole closed the flame is yellow, cooler, and easy to see; with it open the flame becomes a hotter, nearly invisible blue cone used for heating test tubes, boiling liquids, and sterilizing tools. It is named for German chemist Robert Bunsen.
  • Significance: It anchors questions on lab equipment and safe heating, and on boiling (Terms section), which it is used to demonstrate.
  • Connections: Its flame is combustion, a packet topic; its blue cone is where the boiling and evaporation experiments of the packet questions get their heat.
  • Confusables: An alcohol lamp also makes a small flame but burns liquid fuel and is cooler; a hot plate is electric and flameless. The clue "natural gas" or "adjustable air hole" means Bunsen burner.

The periodic table

  • Identify: The periodic table is the chart that organizes all chemical elements, first designed by Dmitri Mendeleev.
  • Explain: Elements are arranged by increasing atomic number - the number of protons (Terms section) - into rows called periods and columns called groups. Elements in a group behave similarly, which is the "periodic" pattern Mendeleev exploited in 1869: arranging by atomic weight and properties, he left gaps and predicted the properties of elements not yet discovered, and he was right. The table sorts elements into metals, nonmetals, and metalloids, and into families such as the halogens and noble gases. The two detached rows at the bottom are the lanthanides and actinides.
  • Significance: It is chemistry's master map: position on the table predicts reactivity, bonding, and physical properties.
  • Connections: Carbon and gold above are entries in it; the proton entry in the Terms explains what atomic number counts, and isotopes (packet) are variants of one entry.
  • Confusables: Rows are periods, columns are groups - reversing them is the classic error. Modern order is by atomic number, not Mendeleev's atomic weight.

Carbon

  • Identify: Carbon is the element with atomic number 6 and symbol C, the basis of organic chemistry.
  • Explain: A carbon atom can form four bonds at once, and can bond to other carbon atoms in chains, rings, and networks, so it builds millions of different compounds - more than all other elements combined. That versatility is why life is carbon-based and why organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. Pure carbon takes several forms, called allotropes: diamond, where atoms lock into a rigid 3D lattice; graphite, where they form slippery layers; and coal, a mostly carbon rock.
  • Significance: Carbon connects chemistry to biology (DNA, proteins, and sugars are carbon frameworks) and to Earth science (diamond, petroleum, and the carbon in the atmosphere).
  • Connections: Diamond gets its own Earth Science entry below; petroleum (Terms) is a carbon-rich fossil fuel; carbon dating in the packet questions uses its isotope carbon-14.
  • Confusables: Carbon the element is not carbon dioxide the compound, and organic chemistry means carbon compounds, not "natural" food labeling.

Computer Science

Pixels

  • Identify: A pixel is the smallest square-shaped element of a raster computer image; the word blends "picture" and "element."
  • Explain: A raster image is a grid of pixels, each storing one color, usually as a mix of red, green, and blue light. Zoom far enough into any photo on a screen and the grid appears. Image resolution is the grid's size in pixels - a 1920 by 1080 screen has about two million of them - and more pixels in the same space means a sharper image.
  • Significance: Pixels are the atom of digital images, so they anchor questions on screens, cameras, and resolution.
  • Connections: The binary code of the packet questions is what stores each pixel's color numbers; artificial intelligence, next, works heavily on pixel grids when it recognizes images.
  • Confusables: A pixel is a unit of an image, not of print size; dots per inch (dpi) describes printing. A vector image, made of shapes and lines, has no pixels until displayed.

Artificial intelligence

  • Identify: Artificial intelligence (AI) is the field of computer science that lets machines perform tasks associated with human intelligence.
  • Explain: AI systems recognize speech and images, translate languages, play games, and generate text and pictures. The dominant modern approach is machine learning: instead of being given explicit rules, a system is trained on large amounts of data and adjusts internal weights until its outputs improve - it learns patterns from experience rather than following only programmed instructions.
  • Significance: AI is the most current topic in the Red guide, and the packet adds its landmarks: the Turing test for judging machine conversation, and the companies and model families of today's systems.
  • Connections: AI runs on algorithms (Math section) executed in binary (packet); the Enigma machine in the Terms was cracked by Alan Turing, who also proposed the Turing test.
  • Confusables: A single clever program is not automatically AI, and machine learning is a subfield of AI, not a synonym for all of it.

Earth Science

Cumulonimbus clouds

  • Identify: Cumulonimbus are tall, dense clouds that produce severe thunderstorms and can spawn tornadoes.
  • Explain: Warm, moist air rising in strong updrafts builds a cumulonimbus from near the ground to the top of the troposphere, where spreading winds flatten it into an anvil top. Inside, colliding ice and water particles build the electric charge that discharges as lightning, and the storms drop heavy rain and hail; the most violent, supercell storms rotate and can lower a tornado.
  • Significance: If a clue mentions an anvil top, hail, lightning, or tornadoes, cumulonimbus is the answer - it is the only cloud type that makes all of them.
  • Connections: Clouds generally are a packet topic (cirrus, stratus, cumulus are the basic types); hurricanes in the packet are built from rings of cumulonimbus around an eye.
  • Confusables: Ordinary cumulus are fair-weather puffs; only the towering cumulonimbus makes severe storms. Stratus makes flat gray sheets and drizzle, not thunder.

Jet streams

  • Identify: Jet streams are fast-moving currents of air high in the atmosphere.
  • Explain: They form near the boundary between the troposphere and stratosphere, where strong temperature contrasts between polar and tropical air drive narrow rivers of wind that can exceed 200 kilometers per hour, generally flowing from west to east. Airliners flying eastbound ride them to cut flight time and fuel use while westbound flights avoid them.
  • Significance: Jet streams steer surface weather systems as well as planes, so they connect upper-air questions to everyday forecasts.
  • Connections: They are the atmospheric counterpart of ocean currents in the packet questions - both are planetary conveyor belts moved by temperature differences and Earth's rotation; convection (Terms) is the engine underneath both.
  • Confusables: A jet stream is air, not water; the Gulf Stream is the famous ocean current. Trade winds are surface winds, not high-altitude currents.

Diamond

  • Identify: A diamond is an allotrope of carbon and the hardest naturally occurring mineral.
  • Explain: Deep in Earth's mantle, extreme heat and pressure force carbon atoms into a rigid three-dimensional lattice in which every atom is bonded to four neighbors; volcanic eruptions then carry diamonds to the surface in special rock pipes. The lattice explains the hardness - 10 on the Mohs scale, the top value -. Graphite, the "lead" of pencils, is the same element arranged in flat layers that slide apart, making it soft enough to write with.
  • Significance: Diamond is the showcase example of how arrangement, not ingredients, determines a material's properties - the key idea of allotropes.
  • Connections: It belongs to carbon in the Chemistry section, and to minerals and crystals in the packet questions, where its Mohs rating and the four C's of grading appear.
  • Confusables: Diamond is hardest, not toughest - it can shatter if struck along a cleavage plane. Quartz (Mohs 7) and cubic zirconia are common look-alikes.

Tsunamis

  • Identify: Tsunamis are powerful ocean waves generated by the displacement of water from a seismic event.
  • Explain: When an undersea earthquake suddenly lifts or drops a slab of seafloor, it displaces the whole column of water above it. In deep ocean the wave is low but extremely long and fast; as it enters shallow coastal water it slows and piles up into a wall of water that can flood far inland. Volcanic eruptions and underwater landslides can trigger them too.
  • Significance: The guide calls them tidal waves, but they have nothing to do with tides - that distinction is itself a favorite question point, along with the earthquake connection.
  • Connections: Their usual cause is the earthquake of the next-but-one entry, driven by plate tectonics (packet); the water cycle's ocean is their medium.
  • Confusables: A storm surge is wind-pushed water from a hurricane and lasts hours at one coast; a tsunami is a series of seismic waves arriving over many hours. Rogue waves are single wind waves.

Archipelago

  • Identify: An archipelago is a chain or cluster of islands.
  • Explain: Many archipelagos are volcanic: a chain of underwater volcanoes rises above the surface one after another, as where a tectonic plate drifts over a hotspot - the Hawaiian Islands grew this way, youngest at one end. Others, like Indonesia, form where plates collide. The Galapagos Islands, a volcanic archipelago on the equator, are famous for the finches that informed Darwin's theory of evolution.
  • Significance: It is the precise term questions want for Hawaii, the Galapagos, Japan, or Indonesia described as island groups.
  • Connections: Archipelagos are built by the volcanoes of this section and the plate movements behind the earthquakes; the Galapagos link back to Darwin in the packet questions.
  • Confusables: An atoll is a ring-shaped coral island around a lagoon, a single feature; an archipelago is the whole group. A peninsula is attached to a mainland and is not an island at all.

Earthquakes

  • Identify: An earthquake is a shaking of the ground, commonly caused by the movement of tectonic plates.
  • Explain: Stress builds where plates push, pull, or slide past each other until the rock suddenly slips along a fault, releasing stored energy as seismic waves. The underground point of the slip is the focus; the epicenter is the point on the surface directly above it. Aftershocks are smaller slips that follow as the crust readjusts. Seismographs record the waves, and strength is reported on the moment magnitude scale.
  • Significance: Earthquakes are the surface expression of plate tectonics, the unifying theory of this whole section - they also trigger tsunamis and mark the Ring of Fire where most volcanoes sit.
  • Connections: Tsunamis (above), volcanoes (below), and plate tectonics (packet) form one system; the Moho boundary in the class notes is found using earthquake waves.
  • Confusables: The epicenter is on the surface, the focus is underground - swapping them is the classic trap. Magnitude measures energy released; intensity measures shaking felt at a place.

Snow

  • Identify: Snow is a solid form of precipitation: soft crystals of ice falling from clouds.
  • Explain: In a cold cloud, water vapor deposits directly onto tiny nuclei and grows into ice crystals with a six-sided structure dictated by the water molecule's geometry. Crystals join into flakes as they fall; if the air stays below freezing all the way down, they arrive as snow rather than melting into rain. Individual flakes are famously varied - no two large, complex flakes are expected to be exactly alike.
  • Significance: Snow is the guide's example of solid precipitation and of deposition, vapor turning straight to solid; it also feeds the glaciers of the packet questions when it accumulates and compacts.
  • Connections: Snow belongs to the water cycle (packet) and to the phase changes of the Terms section - boiling is its liquid-to-gas counterpart at the hot end.
  • Confusables: Sleet is rain that refreezes into pellets on the way down; hail is layered ice built by thunderstorm updrafts; frost forms on surfaces and does not fall.

Volcanoes

  • Identify: Volcanoes are fissures - openings - in a planet's surface that allow ash and lava to escape.
  • Explain: Below the surface, molten rock called magma collects in a chamber; when pressure and dissolved gases build, magma forces up a central vent and erupts. At the surface it is called lava. Runny lava spreads into broad, gentle shield volcanoes like Hawaii's; thick, gas-rich lava piles into steep stratovolcanoes that erupt explosively.   • Connections: Magma cooling underground or lava cooling at the surface makes igneous rock (packet); obsidian in the Terms is volcanic glass from lava that cooled too fast to crystallize.
  • Confusables: Magma is underground, lava is at the surface - the same material, two names. A volcano is the landform and vent; an eruption is the event.

The flat leaf

  • Identify: The flatness of a plant leaf aids its ability to absorb as much sunlight energy as possible for photosynthesis.
  • Explain: A leaf is a solar panel shaped by geometry: spreading a thin blade wide exposes the largest possible area of chlorophyll-containing tissue to the light, while staying thin keeps every cell close to the surface for gas exchange and light. The leaf attaches to the stem by a stalk called the petiole, and its broad surface is also why leaves lose water.
  • Significance: This is the guide's structure-function fact for plants: form follows the physics of catching light, the same logic as the small intestine's villi maximizing absorption area.
  • Connections: Leaves do the photosynthesis described in the packet questions, feed the phloem of the Terms section with the sugar they make, and sit atop the roots of the Terms, which supply their water.
  • Confusables: The stem's job is support and transport, the root's is absorption from soil; only the leaf is specialized for capturing sunlight.

Math

Algorithms

  • Identify: An algorithm is a step-by-step procedure that is guaranteed to solve a given problem.
  • Explain: An algorithm is a finite list of unambiguous instructions that always finishes and always gives a correct result for its problem: a recipe is an everyday example, and sorting a list of numbers from smallest to largest is the classic teaching example. Computer programs are algorithms written in code. The guarantee matters - a method that usually works, or that might loop forever, is a heuristic, not an algorithm.
  • Significance: Algorithms are the bridge between the Math and Computer Science sections: every AI system and every program in the packet's computing questions is built from them.
  • Connections: The square root in the Terms can be found by algorithms; the Enigma codebreaking in the Terms was an algorithmic contest between machines and mathematicians.
  • Confusables: An algorithm is the procedure, a program is its written implementation, and a formula is a single calculation, not a sequence of steps.

Triangles

  • Identify: A triangle is a three-sided geometric figure; its varieties are equilateral, isosceles, and scalene.
  • Explain: By sides: an equilateral triangle has all three sides (and all three angles, 60 degrees each) equal; an isosceles has two equal sides; a scalene has none. By angles: a right triangle has one 90-degree angle, an obtuse triangle one angle over 90 degrees, and an acute triangle all angles under 90. In every triangle the interior angles sum to 180 degrees, and the area is one-half times base times height.
  • Significance: Triangles carry more bee-ready facts than any other shape: the angle sum, the classification names, the area formula, and the Pythagorean theorem and Heron's formula from the packet and class notes.
  • Connections: Pi (next) measures circles the way the angle sum measures triangles; Heron's formula in the class notes computes a triangle's area from its three sides alone.
  • Confusables: Isosceles means exactly two equal sides in most school usage, so an equilateral is the special all-equal case; keep side-based and angle-based names separate, since a triangle has one of each.

Pi

  • Identify: Pi is a transcendental number, approximately 3.14, central to the formulas for circles.
  • Explain: Pi is defined as the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter - measure any circle and divide, and you get the same constant, 3.14159..., with digits that never end and never repeat. It is irrational (it cannot be written as an exact fraction) and, more strongly, transcendental. The area of a circle is pi multiplied by the radius squared, and the circumference is pi times the diameter.
  • Significance: Pi is the most tested constant in school mathematics; questions target its definition as a ratio, its approximate value, and the area formula.
  • Connections: The circle, sphere, and square root entries in the packet and Terms all use pi or its style of definition; the ellipse (Terms) stretches the circle pi describes.
  • Confusables: Area uses radius squared - using the diameter, or forgetting the square, are the standard errors. 22/7 is only an approximation of pi, not its value.

Physics

Radioactivity

  • Identify: Radioactivity is the process by which the unstable nucleus of an atom releases energy.
  • Explain: Some nuclei hold together poorly and spontaneously break down, emitting alpha particles (helium nuclei), beta particles (electrons), or gamma rays (high-energy light). Each emission changes the nucleus, often turning the atom into a different element, at a rate measured by half-life - the time for half of a sample to decay. Henri Becquerel discovered the effect in 1896 (see Terms), and it is harnessed in nuclear reactors and smoke detectors and used in carbon-14 dating.
  • Significance: Radioactivity links the physics of the nucleus to chemistry (isotopes), medicine, and Earth science dating methods.
  • Connections: Becquerel and the Curies are its discoverers in the Terms; isotopes in the packet are its raw material, and relativity's E = mc squared explains where the released energy comes from.
  • Confusables: Radioactivity is not contagious and is not the same as radiation generally - sunlight is radiation but not radioactivity. Fission is a splitting we induce; radioactive decay happens spontaneously.

Friction

  • Identify: Friction is the force resisting motion between two surfaces in contact.
  • Explain: Microscopic bumps and bonds between surfaces oppose sliding - and even the start of sliding - so friction always acts opposite to motion or attempted motion. Rougher surfaces and heavier loads pressing the surfaces together increase it. It is a nuisance that wastes energy as heat in engines, and a necessity everywhere else: tire treads grip the road, brakes stop cars, and walking would be impossible without it. Lubricants like oil reduce it by separating the surfaces.
  • Significance: Friction is the most everyday force in the set and the standard example in Newton's laws questions from the packet.
  • Connections: It is one of the forces in the Terms section's Force entry, and it is the reason simple machines (Terms) never deliver all the work put into them.
  • Confusables: Friction opposes relative motion between surfaces, not motion in general - it is friction that pushes a walking foot forward. Air resistance is a separate drag force from a fluid.

Magnets

  • Identify: A magnet is any object that produces a magnetic field, creating attractive or repulsive force.
  • Explain: Every magnet has two poles, north and north-seeking and south. Opposite poles attract and like poles repel; cut a magnet in half and each piece has both poles - isolated poles do not exist. Its field, pictured by iron filings lining up along field lines, is strongest at the poles. Earth itself is a giant magnet, which is why compass needles align.
  • Significance: Magnets connect electricity and force - the packet's electric current topic meets this one in electromagnets, motors, and generators.
  • Connections: Earth's magnetic field and compasses appear in the packet's class notes; the moons and planets questions use magnetic fields to judge planetary interiors.
  • Confusables: A magnet attracts iron, nickel, and cobalt, not all metals - aluminum and copper are not attracted. Magnetic poles are not electric charges, though the attract-repel rule feels similar.

Acceleration

  • Identify: Acceleration is the rate of change of an object's velocity.
  • Explain: In words, acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time taken, measured in meters per second squared. Because velocity includes direction, acceleration covers three cases: speeding up, slowing down (often called deceleration), and changing direction, as in circular motion. Near Earth's surface, gravity accelerates falling objects at about 9.8 meters per second squared. Newton's second law ties it to force and mass (both in the Terms): force equals mass multiplied by acceleration.
  • Significance: Acceleration is the quantity that makes Newton's laws quantitative, and its units and the 9.8 figure are reliable point-scorers.
  • Connections: Force and mass in the Terms complete the F = ma triangle with this entry; gravity in the packet is the source of the standard 9.8 value.
  • Confusables: Velocity is how fast and in which direction; acceleration is how that velocity changes. A car cruising at a steady 100 km/h has high velocity and zero acceleration.

Terms for Further Study

The guide lists these 30 terms without explanation, expecting you to research them. Each entry below gives the identification, the mechanism or key fact, and the trap to avoid.

  1. DNA - Deoxyribonucleic acid, the double-helix molecule in the cell nucleus that stores genetic instructions in a four-letter code of bases: adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine, with adenine pairing to thymine and guanine to cytosine. Rosalind Franklin's X-ray images enabled Watson and Crick's 1953 model. Trap: RNA is single-stranded and uses uracil instead of thymine.
  2. Phloem - The plant tissue that transports sugars made by photosynthesis from the leaves to roots, fruits, and growing parts, a process called translocation. It works in both directions as needed. Trap: xylem is the partner tissue, carrying water and minerals upward from the roots only.
  3. Trophic levels - The feeding positions in an ecosystem: producers (plants) first, then primary consumers (herbivores), then secondary and tertiary consumers (carnivores). Only about 10 percent of energy passes from one level to the next, which is why food chains are short and top predators are rare.
  4. Nebula - A cloud of gas and dust in space. Some nebulae collapse under gravity to form new stars ("stellar nurseries," like the Orion Nebula, visible as a fuzzy patch below Orion's belt); others are the expanding remains of a supernova. Trap: a nebula is inside a galaxy; it is not itself a galaxy.
  5. Simple machine - A basic device that makes work easier by changing a force's size or direction. There are six classic types, summarized below. None creates energy: each trades distance for force. Trap: a motor or engine is not a simple machine.

  1. Square root - The number that, multiplied by itself, gives the original number: the square root of 9 is 3, and of 25 is 5. It is the inverse operation of squaring. Roots of perfect squares are whole numbers; most others, like the square root of 2, are irrational.
  2. Enigma machine - The German cipher machine of World War II, which used rotating wheels and a plugboard to scramble messages into code, changing the code with every keystroke. Polish mathematicians made the first breaks, and Alan Turing's team at Bletchley Park in England industrialized the cracking, shortening the war.
  3. Ellipse - An elongated closed curve with two focal points (foci), defined by the rule that the sum of distances from any point on it to the two foci is constant. Kepler's first law: every planet orbits in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. A circle is the special ellipse whose foci coincide.
  4. Cancer - A disease in which cells divide uncontrollably, forming malignant tumors that can invade nearby tissue and metastasize - spread through blood or lymph to distant sites. Causes include inherited mutations, smoking, radiation, and some viruses; treatments include surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation. Trap: benign tumors grow in place and do not spread.
  5. Force - A push or pull that can change an object's motion, direction, or shape, measured in newtons. By Newton's second law, force equals mass multiplied by acceleration. Gravity, friction, tension, and magnetism are all forces. Trap: force is measured in newtons, energy in joules.
  6. Henri Becquerel - The French physicist who discovered radioactivity in 1896 by accident: uranium salts he left in a drawer on a wrapped photographic plate fogged the plate anyway, proving the rays came from the uranium itself, not sunlight. He shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with Marie and Pierre Curie, and the SI unit of radioactivity is named the becquerel for him.
  7. Petroleum - A thick liquid fossil fuel, also called crude oil, formed over millions of years from ancient marine organisms buried under sediment and transformed by heat and pressure. Refineries separate it by boiling point into gasoline, diesel, kerosene, and other products. Trap: petroleum is liquid; coal is the solid fossil fuel and natural gas the gaseous one.
  8. Oort cloud - A hypothesized spherical shell of icy bodies surrounding the Solar System at up to about a light-year from the Sun, proposed by Dutch astronomer Jan Oort. It is believed to be the reservoir of long-period comets. Trap: the Kuiper Belt is a closer, flatter disk beyond Neptune - Pluto's neighborhood.
  9. Obsidian - A jet-black volcanic glass formed when lava cools so quickly that crystals have no time to grow. Because it fractures with smooth, curved, razor-sharp edges, ancient peoples chipped it into blades and arrowheads. Trap: obsidian is a glass, not a crystal - its disorder is the whole point.
  10. Prion - An infectious agent made only of protein: a misfolded protein that forces normal copies of the same protein to misfold too. Prions contain no DNA or RNA, are not alive, and cause mad cow disease in cattle and Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease in humans. Trap: a virus has genetic material; a prion has none.
  11. Pterodactyl - Informally, the flying reptiles properly called pterosaurs; Pterodactylus is one famous genus. Their wing was a skin membrane stretched from a greatly elongated fourth finger to the body. They lived alongside dinosaurs through the Mesozoic but were not dinosaurs themselves - a distinction questions love to test.
  12. Roots - The underground plant organs that anchor the plant and absorb water and minerals, growing downward and outward. Some roots, like carrots and beets, also store sugars and starches. They feed water to the xylem, which supplies the leaves whose sugar the phloem then distributes.
  13. Proton - The positively charged particle in an atom's nucleus, found alongside neutrons. The number of protons - the atomic number - determines which element the atom is: 6 protons is always carbon. Trap: electrons carry the negative charge and live outside the nucleus; neutrons are neutral.
  14. Punnett square - A grid diagram, named for British geneticist Reginald Punnett, that predicts the probability of offspring inheriting allele combinations. One parent's alleles label the top, the other's the side; capital letters mark dominant traits and lowercase recessive ones. It gives ratios and probabilities, not guarantees for any single child.
  15. Mass - The amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms; it also measures inertia, the resistance to acceleration, since force equals mass times acceleration. Mass is the same on Earth, the Moon, or in space. Trap: weight is gravity's force on that mass and changes with location.
  16. Andromeda galaxy - The nearest large spiral galaxy to the Milky Way, visible to the naked eye as a faint smudge in the constellation Andromeda. It is larger than the Milky Way and approaching it; the two are expected to merge in a few billion years. Trap: Andromeda is a galaxy of billions of stars, not a nebula or a single star.
  17. Ozone layer - The region of the stratosphere rich in ozone, a molecule of three oxygen atoms, which absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation. Chemicals called chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) thinned it, opening the Antarctic ozone hole; the Montreal Protocol phased CFCs out. Trap: ozone at ground level is a harmful pollutant in smog - altitude changes its role.
  18. Catalyst - A substance that speeds up a chemical reaction by lowering the activation energy it needs to start, without being permanently consumed or changed. Enzymes are the catalysts of living cells. Trap: a catalyst changes how fast a reaction goes, not what it produces, and it emerges unchanged.
  19. Ribosome - The cell organelle that builds proteins: it reads instructions carried by messenger RNA and links amino acids together in the specified order. Ribosomes float free in the cytoplasm or stud the rough endoplasmic reticulum. Because bacteria have them too, several antibiotics work by targeting bacterial ribosomes.
  20. Convection - Heat transfer in a fluid (liquid or gas) by bulk movement: warm, less dense fluid rises while cooler, denser fluid sinks, setting up a circulating current. It boils water in a pot, drives weather, and slowly churns Earth's mantle, moving the tectonic plates. Trap: conduction passes heat by direct contact, radiation by waves with no medium.
  21. Moons of Jupiter - Jupiter has dozens of known moons; the four largest are the Galilean moons, observed by Galileo in 1610: volcanic Io, icy ocean-hiding Europa, Ganymede (the largest moon in the Solar System, bigger than Mercury), and cratered Callisto. Trap: Titan is Saturn's great moon, not Jupiter's.
  22. Relativity - Einstein's pair of theories. Special relativity (1905): the speed of light is the same for all observers, so fast-moving clocks run slow (time dilation) and mass converts to energy by E = mc squared. General relativity (1915): gravity is the curvature of spacetime by mass and energy. Trap: relativity did not overturn Newton for everyday speeds - it extends him.
  23. Boiling - The phase change in which a liquid turns to gas throughout its bulk, with bubbles of vapor forming inside the liquid, not just at the surface. It happens when vapor pressure equals atmospheric pressure - for water, 100 degrees Celsius at sea level, and lower on a mountain. Trap: evaporation is the slow surface-only version at any temperature.
  24. White blood cells - The immune system's cells, also called leukocytes, made in bone marrow. They patrol blood and tissues, engulfing bacteria, producing antibodies, and destroying infected cells; their numbers rise during infection, which is why a blood count helps diagnose illness. Trap: red blood cells carry oxygen; platelets handle clotting.
  25. Trilobite - An extinct marine arthropod with a hard, segmented exoskeleton and compound eyes, among the most common fossils of the Paleozoic era. Trilobites thrived for nearly 270 million years before dying out in the Permian mass extinction about 252 million years ago. Their abundance and variety make them classic index fossils for dating rock layers.
